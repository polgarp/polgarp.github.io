# ☕ [title]

## Beat 1 — a probe is two things

- the made thing, and what comes back
- name the halves; keep them separate for the rest of the issue
- "probe" as the umbrella: an interview, a spike, a map, a spec, a prototype, an
  experiment — all the same kind of object
- » This is the vocabulary beat. e51 had vacation photos, e52 had belief/dogma.
  Establish it early and plainly, don't decorate it yet.
- » Don't introduce frame/distil. Only the move in play.

## Beat 2 — we name probes after the made thing

- "we're building a prototype", "we're running a survey"
- the artifact is what gets scheduled, staffed, reviewed, admired
- Houde & Hill 1997: prototype vocabulary centres on the prototype's own attributes
  — what tool made it, how refined it looks
- Hoang: demos with no learning objective
- » Keep this short and recognisable. It's the setup, not the argument.

## Beat 3 — that was harmless while making was expensive

- cost bound the two halves together
- nobody built the instrument without wanting a reading
- the expense *was* the specification, enforced by whoever signed it off
- Chris Jones / SVPG 2017: in the old failure mode prototypes were too expensive to
  abandon — the opposite of now
- » This is the hinge. It has to be clearly *not* e52's friction paragraph. e52's
  friction surfaced the frame; this friction produced the spec. Say the different
  thing explicitly if there's a risk of it reading as a repeat.

## Beat 4 — making got cheap, the return didn't

- Cagan's cost inversion: live-data prototypes were the expensive kind, now among
  the cheapest — the dimension of realness that used to be rationed is the free one
- Product Circle 2026: engineering 50.2%, design & prototyping 45.3%,
  discovery & research 29.4%
- returns still cost what they cost: contact with users, real data, production,
  the market
- » **This is the mechanism sentence of the whole issue.** Everything before it is
  setup, everything after is consequence.
- » **The obvious objection, and it must be answered in the body, not conceded in a
  clause:** agents made analysis, recruiting, synthesis faster too — that's the
  return half. The defensible line: *processing* got cheaper, *contact with
  something that can contradict you* did not. Synthetic returns are the limit case.
- » The unused e51 line lives here: you can now generate the photos of a trip
  nobody took. It indicts the PM who generates a persona set as much as any agent.

## Beat 5 — so we accumulate made things that returned nothing

- and nobody notices, because the made thing is good
- Paschal: prototypes are now the bare minimum
- the Lenny/Matthews guide as the evidence: a whole craft guide about making,
  "test" once, "user" six times, never a user of the product
- » Verify that count against the live article before printing a number — the vault
  clip looks like it stops at a paywall.
- » This is the beat with the most sting. Don't over-argue it; the artifact-count
  observation does the work.

## Beat 6 — and the reading gets fitted to what we already believed

- nobody agreed in advance what a return would mean
- an agent will produce a plausible reading of any result
- Bakhshi's worked example: interviews say syncing is broken, survey says usefulness
  is high, behaviour says people keep coming back after correcting by hand — ask for
  a synthesis and you get one coherent story that fits
- Mitropoulos et al. 2026: framing code as bug-free cut detection by 16–93 points,
  asymmetrically — false negatives spike, false positives barely move
- » Security paper. Transferring it is an argument, not a citation — say so in half
  a sentence rather than smuggling it.
- » Payoff line available: returns that can't push back aren't returns.

## Beat 7 — so specify the return, not the artifact

- name what comes back before you make the thing
- Bakhshi: what do we expect to learn, and what changes if we get it
- Camuffo et al.: 116 founders, treated arm defined success and failure criteria
  *beforehand*, better performance, more pivots; 759-firm replication adds the
  effect on idea termination
- » The termination finding is the more interesting half — specifying the return in
  advance is what lets a team **stop**. Nothing else in the arc has said that.
- Houde & Hill's own guidance, already written in 1997: know your prototype,
  prepare your audience; prototypes do not communicate their own purpose

## Beat 8 — and decide what has to be real

- what you make real selects which risk you can speak to
- Cagan's three dimensions: visual, behavioural, data — "just enough fidelity"
  depends on the risk being addressed; a feasibility prototype may need no UI
- generated prototypes are spiky: maximal on appearance, near-zero on data, scale,
  edge cases — they can answer *response* and nothing else, however finished
- Ramsden as one axis of the spec (how real, which is also what it cost), not as
  the framework
- » **Cut this beat first if the issue runs long** — it's the one that could become
  its own issue later, and beat 7 survives without it.
- » If it stays: Abraham's objection has to be answered — enumerating fidelity
  combinations before prototyping "feels tedious… an analytical pursuit". Cagan's
  risk-coupling is the only available answer.
- » Houde & Hill separate *resolution* (amount of detail) from *fidelity* (closeness
  to the eventual design). Useful if the word "fidelity" starts doing two jobs.

## Beat 9 — close

- making got cheap, learning didn't
- » The arc premise on a new mechanism. e52's version was ordering (depth got
  cheaper, breadth didn't). This one is composition (the two halves of a single
  probe came apart). Make sure it doesn't land as a restatement.
- » Leadership altitude, per the newsletter's usual register: the thing to protect
  is not the testing but the sentence written before the building starts.

# 🍪 Things to snack on

» Six to eight, matching e51/e52. Ranked shortlist — full summaries and quotes are
  in the research brief, don't rewrite them from memory:

1. **Houde & Hill**, *What do Prototypes Prototype?* (Apple, 1997) — the canonical
   one, unspent, and the only non-practitioner design source in the arc so far.
2. **Marty Cagan**, *The Purpose of Prototypes* (SVPG, 2025) — the fidelity/risk
   coupling and the cost inversion.
3. **Saeideh Bakhshi**, *Stop Automating Research One Task at a Time* (2026) — the
   stopping rule and the pre-registration guard. Also flags issue 5's territory.
4. **Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella & Spina** (Management Science, 2020) + the 2024
   759-firm replication — the empirical anchor, as Boussioux was for e52.
5. **Chris Jones**, *Product Discovery: Pitfalls and Anti-Patterns* (SVPG, 2017) —
   one-dimensional discovery; the tool-first failure named nine years early.
6. **David Hoang**, *The art of prototyping* (2025) — no clear learning objective.
7. **Allie Paschal**, *The hidden cost of AI prototypes that are made to die* (2026)
   — prototypes as the bare minimum, plus the rebuild cost.
8. **Mitropoulos et al.** (arXiv, 2026) — framing bias in LLM review. Use only if
   the transfer from security is stated openly.

» Also available, not shortlisted: Ryan Singer's fidelity curve (better used in the
  body as the thing that breaks), Trys Mudford on fidelity layers, George Abraham on
  story-of-use, Teresa Torres on rapid prototyping (**third arc use — judgement
  call**), Kajsa Westman, Jim Lewis on RITE, Wang & Wu as the counterweight.

» Ramsden is already spent as an e51 snack, so use him in the body instead.
